Use this file in Stages 1, 3 and 5.

- **Part A** holds the cross-cutting patterns. Check them for *every* crisis.

## Part A: Cross-cutting patterns

### A1 Structural fragility
Source: the "September 2026" essay (user-supplied), generalized. Each pattern is a hallmark of "worse or longer than priced". Score each with an LR in Stage 4.

1. **The substitute shares the same risk.** The fallback is exposed to the same shock. Examples:
   - bypass pipelines whose terminals are in range of the same conflict;
   - an alternative supplier in the same drought belt;
   - a second bank funded by the same depositors.

   Markets price the substitute as if it were independent.
2. **Several shocks hit one buffer.** One strategic reserve, one insurer capital pool or one fiscal budget absorbs several crises at once. The buffer looks ample for each shock separately and is not ample for their sum.
3. **Relief is not repair.** Stock releases, subsidies, price caps and talks lower the price without restoring supply. When the relief runs out, a second leg follows. Track physical repair (flows, capacity, inventories), not headlines.
4. **Price caps create shortages.** The problem moves from prices to quantities: queues, rationing, grey markets. Price indices understate the stress, and the eventual decontrol produces a jump.
5. **Biological and physical lags.** Some damage is fixed on a calendar, not by policy:
   - fertilizer shortfalls hit the *next* harvest;
   - culled herds take 1–3 years to rebuild;
   - ships take 2–3 years to build, and fabs and smelters months to requalify.

   Markets tend to price the headline and miss the lag.
6. **A key buyer, guarantor or security provider retreats.** When the actor who stabilized the system steps back, the old equilibrium no longer holds. That actor might be a dominant importer, a naval protector, a lender of last resort or an index buyer.
7. **Near misses come more often.** More close calls per month (a near-collision, a shot across the bow, a narrowly averted default) mean the system has less slack. This is the qualitative twin of critical slowing down (see the early-warning section in signals-and-scoring).

### A2 Financed booms
Source: The Economist, "Nvidia is the central bank of AI" (2026, user-supplied), generalized. Use this for any boom where the seller finances the buyer: AI compute, telecoms in 1999–2001, shipping newbuild cycles, property developers.

- **Vendor financing.** The supplier lends to, invests in or guarantees its customers. Reported demand then overstates end demand.
- **Off-balance-sheet guarantees.** These include residual-value guarantees, take-or-pay backstops and leases through special-purpose vehicles. The risk returns to the sponsor when it is called on.
- **A slowdown, not a collapse, triggers the bust.** Guarantees and covenants trip on *deceleration*: utilization or rental prices dipping below the level the financing assumed. Watch the second derivative.
- **Collateral value depends on scarcity.** Loans secured on scarce assets (GPUs, ships, licences) lose cover when supply catches up. Falling collateral prices then force sales, which push collateral prices lower still.
- **Longevity.** Financed booms last longer than sceptics expect, as long as funding stays open (Abreu & Brunnermeier 2003). The end is set by **funding conditions and the collateral price**, not by valuation.

**What to watch:**
- the borrowers' credit spreads;
- the rental or utilization price of the collateral;
- circular deals, where the supplier invests in the customer that buys from it;
- customer concentration;
- capex funded by debt rather than cash flow.

**Instrument read-through:**
- the supplier's sector ETF (semis, for example) ↓ when it breaks;
- lenders and private-credit vehicles ↓;
- the collateral's own market ↓;
- the customers' bonds ↓.

### A3 Fraud and the timing of being right
Sources: the short-seller documentary (user-supplied: Lernout & Hauspie, Wirecard) and the literature in B7.

- Being right is not enough. Shorts face recalls, squeezes and margin calls; halo effects and protective regulators can last for years (Shleifer & Vishny 1997, limits of arbitrage).
- Prices correct when a **public, synchronizing catalyst** forces everyone to act at once (Abreu & Brunnermeier 2003). Examples: a special audit, an auditor's refusal to sign, a missed filing deadline, a raid, a court ruling.
- **No catalyst in view means no position, or a token one.**

## Part B: Families

### B1 Conflict, sanctions and chokepoints

**Earliest hard signals:**
- **Insurance and shipping:** war-risk premiums; changes to the Joint War Committee listed areas; UKMTO and JMIC advisories.
- **Traffic:** AIS transit counts against their own baseline (IMF PortWatch); NOTAMs and airspace closures.
- **Mobilization:** embassy drawdowns and evacuations; reserve call-ups.
- **Sanctions:** designations of shipping companies.
- **Indices:** the GPR index, with *threats* and *acts* read separately (Caldara & Iacoviello 2022); the GDELT Goldstein tone against the region's own baseline (Mueller & Rauh 2018).

**Escalation-trap checklist.** These are hallmarks that the conflict persists or deepens. Each "yes" is a candidate LR.
- **No face-saving exit.** Both sides have stated maximal aims in public, so backing down carries high audience costs (Fearon 1994).
- **Commitment problem.** A side fears the other will be stronger later, or concessions can't be credibly guaranteed (Fearon 1995). Long wars are mostly commitment-problem wars (Weisiger 2013).
- **Indivisible stakes:** regime survival, sacred territory, or nuclear status.
- **Mutual optimism.** Both sides claim to be winning, and battlefield information stays ambiguous.
- **Spoilers.** Proxies or factions act with their own agency and can restart the fighting.
- **Gambling for resurrection.** Leaders of mixed, moderately exclusionary regimes face severe punishment for a moderate loss, so they keep fighting losing wars. Democracies settle sooner (Goemans 2000).
- **Escalating targets.** Strikes move up the ladder toward energy, water, ports and economic infrastructure.
- **Commercial withdrawal.** Owners won't sail even with naval escorts. Insurance and crews are the binding constraint, not the navy.
- **Substitutes share the risk** (A1.1), and buffers are drained by stacked shocks (A1.2).

**Termination checklist.** These are counter-signals. When several line up, timing row 9 reads late and a thesis-break exit is near.
- **Ripeness** (Zartman 2000) needs all three of:
  - (i) a *mutually hurting stalemate*: costs rising for both sides and no path to escalating toward victory;
  - (ii) a *way out*: a formula or mediator both sides can accept;
  - (iii) *valid spokesmen* who can deliver their side.

  One or two of these alone don't make it ripe.
- **Convergence** (Slantchev 2003). Fighting stops revealing new information: front lines and blockade status are stable, and the sides' stated terms move toward each other.
- **Leadership change.** New leaders who are not tied to the war settle more readily (Goemans 2000). A hardliner's resignation is ambiguous; check who replaces them.
- **A third-party guarantor emerges.** Enforcement solves the commitment problem (Walter 2002).
- **Costly de-escalation signals:** prisoner exchanges, mine clearance, accepted inspection or escort regimes, sanctions relief that has been sequenced.
- **Market tells:**
  - war-risk premiums falling;
  - transits rising for 2+ weeks;
  - the freight forward curve steepening into backwardation, meaning the market expects normalization.

**Blind spots:**
- Acts that do not impair supply spike prices and then revert; GPR *acts* spikes mean-revert.
- Equities have often bottomed near the start of a war (1991, 2003).
- The edge is in *sustained mechanisms*, not in the opening headline.

**Instrument ladder:**

| Rung | Instruments | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | Brent/WTI ETCs; gas (TTF) ETCs; gold ETCs | ↑ |
| Direct | Freight: tanker and container rates | ↑ (but there is **no UCITS freight ETF**; use the tanker or container shipping stocks that gain from longer ton-miles) |
| Sector | Energy producers outside the conflict zone; defence (UCITS defence ETFs) | ↑ |
| Sector | Airlines and travel; European chemicals (feedstock and energy costs) | ↓ |
| Sector | Insurers | mixed: premiums ↑, claims ↓ |
| Second-order | Refiners (crack spreads); LNG shippers; fertilizer producers outside the zone | ↑ |
| Second-order | Import-dependent Asian equities and FX (India, Korea, Japan); gas-intensive European industry | ↓ |
| Second-order | Next-season food prices | ↑ (through the fertilizer lag) |

**Analogues and durations:**
- **1973 embargo:** the posted price roughly quadrupled within months and stayed higher after the embargo ended in Mar 1974.
- **1980s tanker war:** years of attacks, with only moderate price impact thanks to spare capacity.
- **1990 Kuwait invasion:** oil roughly doubled between Aug and Oct 1990, then collapsed on 17 Jan 1991 when the air war began, falling about a third in a day.
- **Russia 2022:** wheat peaked in early March and was back near pre-war levels by about July, after the Black Sea grain deal of 22 Jul 2022. European gas (TTF) peaked in late Aug 2022.
- **Red Sea 2023–25:** carriers rerouted from mid-Dec 2023. The Drewry WCI went from about $1.4k to $3.9k per FEU by Jan 2024 and peaked around $5.9k in Jul 2024, and rerouting lasted more than a year.

- "transits fell" strait
- "Joint War Committee" listed areas
- "naval escort" shipping insurers
- "embassy" evacuate dependents
- "red line" [leader]
- "sanctions" tankers designated
- "talks collapse" OR "ceasefire proposal" mediator

### B2 Weather and climate

**Earliest hard signals:**
- **Seasonal outlooks:** NOAA CPC ENSO advisories (El Niño/La Niña watch); ECMWF seasonal forecasts; monsoon onset.
- **Crops and soil:** drought monitors and soil moisture; USDA weekly crop-condition ratings.
- **Hurricanes:** NHC outlooks and track cones.
- **Rivers:** the Rhine at Kaub, where low water halts barges.
- **Other:** snowpack; heatwave forecasts that drive power demand.

**Hallmarks of worse or longer than priced:**
- The weather hits a **concentrated producer**:
  - cocoa, where Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana grow roughly 60% of the world's supply;
  - coffee (Brazil, Vietnam);
  - orange juice (Florida, Brazil);
  - soy (Brazil, US).
- **Multi-season effects.** Perennial crops (trees) carry damage into later years, and El Niño can roll into a second year.
- **Weather and disease together:** cocoa swollen shoot, citrus greening.
- A **low stocks-to-use** ratio.
- **Export bans follow**, triggering the export-ban multiplier (Giordani et al. 2016).
- **Repeated infrastructure damage:** Gulf Coast refineries, successive Rhine low-water years.
- **Slow trends.** Markets underreact to gradual drought trends; food-company returns are predictable from drought indices (Hong, Li & Xu 2019).

**Blind spots:**
- Forecasts themselves are priced fast. Orange-juice futures anticipate temperature forecast errors (Roll 1984).
- Options price hurricane uncertainty around landfall (Kruttli, Roth Tran & Watugala, "Pricing Poseidon").
- So the edge is in **persistence and biology**, not in the forecast itself (inference).
- Insurers often drop on the event and then rise as premiums harden.

**Instrument ladder:**

| Rung | Instruments | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | Agricultural ETCs (cocoa, coffee, sugar, wheat, corn, soybeans); natural gas ETCs for heat or cold | ↑ |
| Sector | Reinsurers after a big loss year | ↑ (hard market) |
| Sector | Primary insurers exposed to the event region | ↓ (short-run) |
| Sector | Food processors and confectioners whose input costs rise | ↓ |
| Second-order | Fertilizer and crop-input producers | ↑ |
| Second-order | Chemicals and coal logistics on low-water rivers; utilities with hydro shortfalls | ↓ |
| Second-order | Exporters' currencies (e.g., BRL with coffee and soy) | mixed |

**Analogues and durations:**
- **El Niño** typically runs 9–12 months and raises non-fuel commodity prices and inflation in many countries (Cashin, Mohaddes & Raissi 2017).
- **Cocoa 2023–24:** prices rose about four- to fivefold from early 2023 to a Dec 2024 peak, then fell back sharply in 2025 (verify).
- **Coffee:** the Jul 2021 Brazil frost, then arabica at a record above $4/lb in early 2025.
- **Katrina/Rita 2005:** refinery outages kept gasoline cracks elevated for weeks.
- **Rhine low water 2018:** stress lasted months (Aug–Dec).
- **Texas freeze Feb 2021:** a gas price spike lasting days.
- **US drought 2012:** corn hit a record around Aug 2012.

**Scan queries:**
- "El Niño watch" OR "La Niña advisory"
- "crop condition" [crop] week
- "frost" Brazil coffee
- "Kaub" Rhine water level
- "hurricane" landfall refinery
- drought [exporter] "export ban"
- "reinsurance renewal" rates

### B3 Government change and policy shocks

**Earliest hard signals:**
- **Elections:** polls and prediction-market probabilities; coalition arithmetic; platform documents.
- **Policy pipeline:** draft bills and consultations; executive-order trackers; tariff notices and their *effective dates*; trade-remedy filings.
- **Strategic goods:** export-control announcements on critical minerals or technology.
- **Market stress gauges:** sovereign CDS; FX implied volatility.
- **Threats** to central-bank independence.

**Hallmarks of worse or longer than priced:**
- The policy is **enacted in law** or takes effect, rather than merely being announced.
- **Retaliation cycles**, for example with tariffs.
- **Institutional erosion:** central-bank independence, courts, statistics agencies.
- **Fiscal dominance**, with bond-market stress.
- **Capital controls**, or **expropriation** of a sector.
- **Export controls by a dominant supplier** (China on rare earths, gallium and germanium; Indonesia on nickel ore). Substitution takes years.
- **Secondary sanctions.**
- **Polarization after a financial crisis** keeps policy uncertainty high (Funke, Schularick & Trebesch 2016).
- **Populist governments** are a slow drag: ~10% lower GDP after 15 years (Funke et al. 2023). That is a context factor, not a trade.

**Blind spots:**
- Election outcomes reprice within days (Wagner, Zeckhauser & Ziegler 2018).
- Announced tariffs are often paused or reversed when markets or bonds sell off. The April 2025 tariff shock, followed by a 90-day pause a week later, is the case in point.
- Fade policy spikes that have not been enacted.

**Instrument ladder:**

| Rung | Instruments | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | The country's MSCI UCITS ETF; its currency; its government-bond ETFs | ↓ on fiscal-dominance fears |
| Sector | Sector winners and losers by policy (banks under deregulation or a steeper curve; renewables under subsidy cuts; defence; healthcare under drug pricing; builders) | depends on the policy |
| Sector | Strategic-metals producers outside China, under export controls (e.g., the VanEck Rare Earth & Strategic Metals UCITS ETF) | ↑ |
| Second-order | Gold, on institutional erosion | ↑ |
| Second-order | Supply chains that depend on the restricted input | ↓ |

**Analogues and durations:**
- **Brexit, Jun 2016:** GBP fell about 10–15% within months and stayed lower for years.
- **Truss mini-budget, Sep 2022:** a gilt and LDI crisis lasting about 4 weeks, ended by Bank of England intervention and a policy reversal.
- **US–China trade war 2018–19:** escalation over about 18 months.
- **Argentina's 2019 primary shock:** the Merval fell about 48% in USD in one day.
- **China's rare-earth quotas 2010–11:** prices spiked several-fold into mid-2011 and collapsed in 2012 through substitution, stockpile release and demand destruction. That is the template for export-control spikes.

**Scan queries:**
- "export controls" [mineral]
- tariff "effective date"
- "central bank independence" [country]
- "snap election" OR "coalition talks collapse"
- "nationalize" OR "windfall tax" [sector]
- "price cap" [sector]
- "capital controls"

### B4 Pandemics

**Earliest hard signals:**
- **Alerts:** WHO Disease Outbreak News; ProMED; unusual pneumonia clusters.
- **Spread:** exported cases detected abroad, which imply a large hidden epidemic at the source; the doubling time (`signals.py doubling`).
- **Virus data:** genomic sequences; early estimates of severity.
- **Response:** lockdown of a major city; school closures.
- **Attention:** symptom searches.

**Hallmarks of worse or longer than priced:**
- sustained human-to-human transmission is confirmed;
- exported cases appear in several countries;
- the doubling time is under a week;
- spread is asymptomatic or presymptomatic;
- there is no vaccine or antiviral;
- officials minimize or censor;
- healthcare capacity comes under strain;
- the outbreak reaches dense transport hubs.

**Exponential-growth bias:** the public and markets underestimate compounding (Stango & Zinman 2009). Prices moved first on China exposure, then globally from 24 Feb 2020 (Ramelli & Wagner 2020).

**Blind spots:**
- Most alerts fizzle: SARS (contained in about 4 months), MERS, Zika, H1N1 2009 (mild), mpox 2022.
- Markets bottom before the epidemic peaks once policy backstops arrive (23 Mar 2020).

**Instrument ladder:**

| Rung | Instruments | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | Travel and leisure; airlines; hotels and cruise lines | ↓ |
| Direct | Healthcare, biotech and vaccine makers | ↑ |
| Sector | Stay-at-home sectors (e-commerce, software) | ↑ |
| Sector | Oil (demand shock) | ↓ |
| Sector | Government bonds; gold | ↑ |
| Recovery | Reopening trades rotate back, as on the vaccine news of 9 Nov 2020 | ↑ for the earlier losers |

**Durations:**
- the COVID crash took about 5 weeks (S&P −34% in 23 trading days);
- sector divergence lasted about a year;
- cruise lines took years to recover.

**Scan queries:**
- "unexplained pneumonia" cluster
- "human-to-human transmission"
- "Disease Outbreak News"
- "exported case" [virus]
- outbreak "doubling time"
- lockdown [city]

### B5 Animal and plant disease

**Earliest hard signals:**
- WOAH WAHIS immediate notifications;
- USDA APHIS confirmations;
- national veterinary agencies;
- cull counts;
- import bans by major buyers.

For plants, watch:
- banana Fusarium TR4;
- cocoa swollen shoot;
- citrus greening (HLB);
- wheat rust.

**Hallmarks of worse or longer than priced:**
- Spread into a **top producer or exporter**. In 2018, ASF hit China, which then held about half the world's pigs.
- **No vaccine.** There is none commercially for ASF, and HPAI vaccination is limited by trade rules.
- A **wildlife reservoir** brings seasonal recurrence: wild boar, migratory birds.
- A **species jump**, such as H5N1 into US dairy cattle (Mar 2024).
- **Biological rebuild lags:**
  - sow herds take about a year;
  - layers take about 5 months to start laying;
  - cattle herds take years.
- **Multi-year import bans.** After BSE, Japan and Korea restricted US beef for years.
- Official underreporting.

**Blind spots:**
- Egg price spikes reverse fast when outbreaks pause. US wholesale eggs fell by more than half within about 2 months of the early-2025 peak.
- Outbreaks in non-exporters matter little.

**Instrument ladder:**

| Rung | Instruments | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | Livestock ETCs (lean hogs, live cattle) | ↑ |
| Direct | Agricultural ETCs (cocoa, coffee, OJ where available) for plant disease | ↑ |
| Sector | Protein producers *outside* the affected area; exporters | ↑ |
| Sector | Producers inside the affected area | ↓ |
| Second-order | Feed grains and soymeal (culled herds eat less; ASF cut China's soy demand) | ↓ |
| Second-order | Animal-health and vaccine makers (single stocks) | ↑ |

**Analogues and durations:**
- **ASF China 2018–19:** pork stayed elevated for about 14 months and took about 38 months to normalize (USDA ERS).
- **US HPAI 2022–25:** egg prices spiked repeatedly.
- **BSE, US, Dec 2003:** cattle futures fell limit-down for days; export bans lasted years.
- **FMD, UK 2001.**
- **Citrus greening:** Florida orange output fell about 90% from its late-1990s peak over two decades, with hurricanes contributing. A slow burn.

**Scan queries:**
- "African swine fever" [country]
- "avian influenza" "commercial flock"
- WOAH "immediate notification"
- "import ban" pork OR beef OR poultry
- H5N1 dairy
- "swollen shoot" cocoa
- "citrus greening"

### B6 Supply-chain breaks

**Earliest hard signals:**
- **Indices:** the NY Fed GSCPI; PMI supplier delivery times and backlogs.
- **Freight:** Drewry WCI, Freightos FBX, Baltic Dry.
- **Ports:** congestion seen through AIS and PortWatch.
- **Supplier notices:** force-majeure declarations; allocation notices.
- **Single-source accidents:** plant fires (Renesas Naka 2021).
- **Prices and lead times:** chip lead times; spot component prices.

**Hallmarks of worse or longer than priced:**
- **Specific, hard-to-substitute inputs** propagate; generic ones don't (Barrot & Sauvagnat 2016).
- **Single-supplier concentration:** leading-edge foundry, lithography, specialty gases (Ukrainian neon in 2022).
- **Long restarts:** fab requalification, smelter restarts.
- **Network propagation.** After the Tōhoku earthquake, shocks spread to suppliers' customers and beyond (Carvalho et al. 2021). US affiliates of Japanese firms cut output roughly one-for-one with lost imports (Boehm, Flaaen & Pandalai-Nayar 2019).
- **Lean inventories.**

**Blind spots:**
- **Short blockages fade fast.** Ever Given blocked Suez for 6 days in Mar 2021 with little lasting effect.
- **The bullwhip reverses.** Orders amplify upstream, so double-ordering turns a shortage into a glut within 12–24 months (Lee, Padmanabhan & Whang 1997). Chips went from shortage in 2021 to glut in 2023, and container rates collapsed in 2022–23. **This is the exit signal for shortage trades.**

**Instrument ladder:**

| Rung | Instruments | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | Producers of the scarce input outside the disrupted region (semis, specialty materials) | ↑ |
| Direct | Container shipping, via stocks | ↑ |
| Direct | Industrial-metal ETCs | ↑ |
| Sector | Downstream assemblers (autos, electronics) | ↓ (output cuts, margins) |
| Sector | Logistics | ↑ early, ↓ in the glut |

**Scan queries:**
- "force majeure" [input]
- supplier "allocation"
- "lead times" chips
- plant fire [component]
- "port congestion"
- PMI "supplier delivery times"
- "shortage" [component]

### B7 Corporate failure and fraud

**Earliest hard signals.** Distress:
- **Fundamentals:** low profitability, high leverage, low cash, high volatility and a low price. Distressed stocks *underperform*; the market does not reward distress risk (Campbell, Hilscher & Szilagyi 2008).
- **Debt markets:** CDS widening; bonds below 80; covenant waivers; rating downgrades.
- **Filings and gatekeepers:** going-concern warnings; auditor resignations; delayed filings; CFO departures.
- **Short interest rising.** It is informative ahead of misconduct revelations (Karpoff & Lou 2010).
- **Who detects fraud.** Fraud is mostly exposed by employees, the media, industry regulators and short sellers, rather than by auditors or securities regulators (Dyck, Morse & Zingales 2010).

**Fraud red flags** (from the documentary and the literature):
- growth far above peers through opaque third parties, or through acquisitions in distant jurisdictions;
- cash held in trustee or escrow accounts that auditors can't confirm directly;
- receivables or "other assets" growing faster than revenue; weak cash conversion;
- frequent auditor or CFO changes; related-party deals;
- **attacks on critics** (lawsuits against journalists, or regulators investigating short sellers instead of the company: BaFin's Feb 2019 short ban on Wirecard);
- a **national-champion halo**;
- **index inclusion forcing passive buying** (Wirecard joined the DAX in Sept 2018).

**Timing:**
- Short theses need a catalyst; see A3.
- **Lernout & Hauspie, 2000:** WSJ reporting on Korean revenue, then an audit review, then bankruptcy in Nov 2000.
- **Wirecard:**
  - FT investigations from Jan 2019;
  - the KPMG special audit (Apr 2020) failed to verify the business;
  - EY refused to sign on 18 Jun 2020;
  - the insolvency filing came on 25 Jun 2020.

**Contagion or competition:**
- Bankruptcies hurt industry rivals on average. Rivals gain in concentrated, low-leverage industries (Lang & Stulz 1992).
- Reorganizations tend to spread credit contagion; liquidations tend to benefit competitors (Jorion & Zhang 2007).
- It is **systemic** only through:
  - counterparty links (Lehman; Archegos and its prime brokers);
  - a shared business-model weakness. SVB pulled in regional banks with unrealized bond losses and uninsured deposits.

**ETF relevance:**
- Single-name fraud rarely moves broad ETFs. Check its weight in concentrated thematic or sector funds the user holds.
- The tradeable effects are:
  - sector contagion overshoot, so buy the sector after the backstop (repair);
  - competitors that benefit, through the competition effect;
  - index-removal flows.

**Analogues:**
- Enron 2001;
- WorldCom 2002;
- Lehman Sep 2008;
- Archegos Mar 2021;
- Evergrande 2021 onwards (years);
- FTX Nov 2022;
- SVB, Signature and First Republic, Mar–May 2023: US regional bank stocks fell roughly 30–40%;
- Credit Suisse Mar 2023.

**Scan queries:**
- "auditor resigns"
- "delayed annual report" OR "going concern"
- "special audit"
- "short seller report"
- whistleblower [company]
- "covenant waiver" OR "distressed exchange"
- "deposit outflows"

### B8 Credit bubbles and lax regulation

**Earliest hard signals:**
- **Credit plus asset prices.** Credit growth and asset-price growth in the top tercile together carry a ~40% chance of crisis within 3 years (Greenwood, Hanson, Shleifer & Sørensen 2022).
- **Credit-to-GDP gap.** The BIS gap above ~10pp is the upper Basel countercyclical-buffer calibration point.
- **Lending standards** loosening (Senior Loan Officer Survey).
- **Issuance quality:** the share of junk issuance (Greenwood & Hanson 2013); the share of covenant-lite loans; payment-in-kind usage in private credit.
- **Fast-growing lenders.** A bank's own fast loan growth predicts poor returns (Fahlenbrach, Prilmeier & Stulz 2018).
- **Securitization of a new asset class** with originate-to-distribute incentives. Credit expanded into subprime zip codes with *falling* relative incomes (Mian & Sufi 2009), and securitization led to lax screening (Keys et al. 2010).
- **Regulatory arbitrage:** ABCP conduits with bank liquidity guarantees amounted to "securitization without risk transfer" (Acharya, Schnabl & Suarez 2013).
- **Shadow-bank growth.**

**Hallmarks of worse or longer than priced:**
- off-balance-sheet guarantees come back onto balance sheets (A2);
- mark-to-model valuations;
- early payment defaults in the weakest vintage;
- short-term wholesale funding: a run on repo through higher haircuts (Gorton & Metrick 2012);
- **a slowdown, not a collapse, trips the triggers**;
- rating-agency lag;
- officials call it "contained";
- bank failures impair intermediation, which deepens and prolongs the downturn (Bernanke 1983).

**Bank equity does not price this.** After a 95th-percentile credit expansion, the predicted 3-year excess return on bank stocks is −37.3% (Baron & Xiong 2017).

**Canonical timeline (subprime).** This is the clearest "months, not days" case: about 18 months from the first public signs to peak stress.
1. The ABX subprime indices weakened from late 2006 and early 2007.
2. HSBC's subprime warning: Feb 2007.
3. New Century's bankruptcy: 2 Apr 2007.
4. The Bear Stearns hedge funds: Jun–Jul 2007.
5. BNP Paribas froze funds: 9 Aug 2007.
6. Northern Rock: Sep 2007.
7. Bear Stearns: Mar 2008.
8. Lehman: 15 Sep 2008.
9. The equity trough: Mar 2009.

**Instrument ladder:**

| Rung | Instruments | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | Bank sector ETFs (STOXX Europe 600 Banks; MSCI World/US Financials); high-yield bond ETFs | ↓ |
| Sector | Homebuilders, REITs, consumer lenders | ↓ |
| Second-order | Government-bond duration; gold | ↑ |
| Second-order | Cyclicals | ↓ |
| After the backstop (repair) | Banks | ↑ |

**Current-era watchlist** (verify that each is still relevant):
- private credit: PIK share, redemption gates, marks;
- commercial real estate;
- AI-capex financing (A2);

### B9 Crashes, recessions and depressions

**Earliest hard signals:**
- **Yield curve:** the 10y–3m inversion (Estrella & Mishkin 1998), then the re-steepening, which often comes near the start of a recession.
- **Credit:** the excess bond premium (Gilchrist & Zakrajšek 2012); credit spreads breaking out of their range.
- **Labour and activity:** the Sahm rule (confirmation, not a lead); the trend in jobless claims; PMI new orders below inventories.
- **Crash risk:**
  - run-up and acceleration (Greenwood, Shleifer & You 2019);
  - turnover with negative skew (Chen, Hong & Stein 2001);
  - leverage (margin debt);
  - feedback hedging: portfolio insurance in 1987, the short-volatility products of Feb 2018;
  - index concentration.

**What turns a recession into a depression (worse or longer):**
- a **banking crisis** (Reinhart & Rogoff 2009: longer and deeper);
- a **prior credit boom** (Jordà, Schularick & Taylor 2013);
- a **policy error**:
  - tightening into a downturn (1931, 1937);
  - gold-standard constraints; countries that left gold earlier recovered sooner (Eichengreen & Sachs 1985);
- **debt deflation** (Fisher 1933);
- a **balance-sheet recession**: Japan in the 1990s, and US household deleveraging (Mian & Sufi).

**Blind spots:**
- Crashes without credit or bank involvement recover fast. After 1987, the S&P regained its prior high in about 2 years. In 2020 it took about 5 months with backstops.
- Early recession calls miss late-cycle gains.
- Stocks usually bottom months before a recession ends.

**Instrument ladder:**

| Rung | Instruments | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | Equity indices; small caps; cyclicals; credit | ↓ |
| Sector | Defensives (staples, healthcare, utilities) | ↑ relative |
| Second-order | Government-bond duration | ↑ in a demand bust, but **↓ in an inflationary bust** (2022) |
| Second-order | Gold | ↑ |

**Analogues:**
- **1929–32:** the Dow fell about 89%, with bank failures and policy error.
- **1973–74:** the S&P fell about 48% amid the oil shock and inflation.
- **1987:** the S&P fell 20.5% in one day (portfolio insurance) and recovered in about 2 years.
- **Japan from 1990:** the Nikkei fell about 80% to 2009 and did not regain its 1989 peak until 2024.
- **2000–02:** the Nasdaq fell about 78% over about 2.5 years.
- **2007–09:** the S&P fell about 57%.
- **2020:** the S&P fell about 34% in 23 trading days.
- **2022 rates shock:** the S&P fell about 25%, and bonds also fell, by about 13% for the year.
